# Bonfire gating: should levelling and shopping require a rest?

Research notes for the house-rules revision of Dark Souls: The Board Game. Gathered 16 November 2026 from open web pages. Quotes are as retrieved; anything second-hand or unverified is marked.

Note on evidence quality: the strongest evidence is the official rulebook text (items 1.1 to 1.3). The 2017 rules text comes from a retailer-hosted PDF of the core rules. The BoardGameGeek and Reddit threads could not be opened (see "Could not reach"), so reception evidence is thinner than it ought to be.

## 1. What the official rules say

### 1.1 2017 core rules (V1): shop and levelling are free between encounters

Source: Steamforged Games, *Dark Souls: The Board Game* core rulebook (2017), PDF hosted at https://gamers-hq.de/media/pdf/97/3a/29/DS-Core-Rules-lores.pdf (text extracted from the PDF, pp. 13 to 15).

- Home base, p. 13: "the characters may return to it [the Bonfire tile] at any time they are not in an encounter."
- Andre, p. 14: "When the party returns to the bonfire between encounters, they may visit Blacksmith Andre to purchase treasure, change equipment, and upgrade equipment. To purchase treasure, remove 1 soul from the soul cache... The only limit to the number of times players may purchase treasure is the number of souls in the soul cache." Upgrading has no soul cost.
- Firekeeper, p. 15: "The party may visit the Firekeeper to level up and restore luck." Costs: Base to Tier 1 is 2 souls, Tier 1 to 2 is 4, Tier 2 to 3 is 8.
- Resting, p. 15: "When the party is defeated in an encounter, the characters must rest at the bonfire. The party may also choose to rest at the bonfire any time they are on the Bonfire tile." A rest turns the spark dial down one, refills Estus, Heroic Action and Luck tokens, and turns all encounter cards face down. Visiting Andre or the Firekeeper is described separately from resting; the rules text does not tie either to the spark cost.
- Soul loss, p. 19: on defeat "place all soul tokens from the soul cache on the node where the character was killed... To retrieve souls from a node, one character must move onto the node where the souls were dropped. If a character dies before these souls are retrieved, the souls are discarded."
- Spark count: 5, 4, 3, 2 sparks for 1 to 4 players (p. 8). Luck can also be restored at the Firekeeper for 1 soul without a rest.

The community "Unofficial Guide & FAQ" (Google Doc, https://docs.google.com/document/d/1DmbTaM68OoosZcSCFW0bTNgBWCaHSP2UE9Um-X4XF54/) states the same reading: "When can I access the Firekeeper to level up? V1: Between encounters without spending a spark. V2: Only when resting at the bonfire." and "When can I access the Blacksmith? V1: Between encounters. V2: Only when resting at the bonfire." Its comparison table adds: V1, "You can visit the bonfire in between encounters to level up, upgrade stats, buy treasure... without spending a spark"; V2, "You spend a spark in order to return to the bonfire, which allows you to upgrade stats, buy treasure..." (community document, not official).

Consequence in V1 (my reading of the text): souls are only at risk inside an encounter (a defeat drops them), and a visit to Andre or the Firekeeper between encounters banks them without cost.

### 1.2 2022 revised core sets (V2): every return costs a spark

Source: Steamforged Games, *Dark Souls: The Sunless City* rulebook, https://steamforged.com/cdn/shop/files/DS-TSC-Rulebook-download-min.pdf (pp. 25 to 27 in the extracted text).

- "After completing an encounter, the players can return to the bonfire instead of moving to the next space on the encounter track. If the players failed the encounter they won't have a choice, they'll have to return to the bonfire."
- "The bonfire is a safe haven where characters can rest for a short time. When they do so, they can access their inventory, and if they have souls to spend, visit Blacksmith Andre and the Firekeeper."
- Resting: "When the party returns to the bonfire, resolve the following steps... 2. Turn the spark dial down one number. 3. Flip each character's heroic action, Estus Flask, and luck tokens to the ready side. 4. Return each encounter card to its normal orientation." The party can then visit the Firekeeper and/or Andre.
- Levelling, in the stats section: "Characters can only upgrade when they rest at the bonfire."
- Andre: draw four treasure cards, buy any number at one soul each, "This step can only be done once per visit to the bonfire."
- Resuming: "Because all of the completed encounter cards have been reset, the players will have to complete them once more in order to progress... By replaying encounters, players can collect the rewards for completing them again too."
- Losing: "Remember, the party cannot visit the bonfire indefinitely. Each time they return to the bonfire, they'll use up a spark. If the spark dial has reached 0, and the players return to the bonfire, they lose the game."
- Soul loss in campaign play: on a failed encounter, souls in the cache are placed on the failed encounter card (the Sunless City campaign text: "Place any souls in the soul cache on the encounter card the players failed"), and tokens on any other encounter card are discarded.
- Sparks as in V1 by player count; the same rules also give a spark back for killing a boss ("Increase the spark dial by 1"), and event cards can grant a free visit ("visit a shop without spending a spark", per the community guide's description of event cards).

Publisher's stated reason for revising the original: Steamforged blog, "Dark Souls: The Board Game, The New Campaign System", https://steamforged.com/blogs/brands/dark-souls%E2%84%A2-the-board-game-the-new-campaign-system: "the most optimum way to play the game was to farm souls and treasure by using each spark available, before facing the boss", and "If your party is overcome, or wants to level up, you'll still return to the bonfire, and reset the encounters." They describe a "natural grind cycle of players repeatedly playing linear encounters against the same enemies". The post does not mention a cost for a voluntary visit.

### 1.3 Summary table

| Item | 2017 (V1) | 2022 (V2) |
|---|---|---|
| Visit Andre | Between encounters, no spark | Only when resting; costs a spark |
| Level up at Firekeeper | Between encounters, no spark | Only when resting; costs a spark |
| Buying treasure | Draw top card blind for 1 soul, any number of times | Four visible cards, 1 soul each, once per visit |
| Restore Luck | 1 soul at Firekeeper without a rest | Only by resting or a reward |
| Encounters reset | Only on a rest (spark) | On every return |
| Spark return after a boss | Refilled to full (per community guide) | Plus one only (per community guide) |

The last row is from the community guide, not checked against the rulebooks beyond the "Increase the spark dial by 1" line in the Sunless City text.

## 2. Reception of the 2022 change

Direct evidence is limited. No source found isolates "shopping and levelling cost a spark" as a topic.

- Unofficial Guide & FAQ (community document), "V1 vs. V2: Key Differences": the V2 campaign gives varied objectives, "However, the grindy nature of repeating the same encounters after restarting at the bonfire is still very much present there." It also says V2 had "Reduced encounter difficulty, partially due to exploitable rules, can make the game less engaging while not really reducing the overall playtime." It calls V2 "V1.1" in spirit.
- BoardGameQuest, review of *Painted World of Ariamis* (V2), https://boardgamequest.com/dark-souls-the-board-game-painted-world-of-ariamis-review (via fetch summary): defeat means players "return to the bonfire, spend a spark, and start over"; replaying encounters "knowing what to expect, to earn extra loot" is called "the core Dark Souls feeling" and linked to "the push-your-luck feel of the source material"; reviewer notes replays can make the game easier over time. Reviewer's main complaints were errata and player boards, not the spark cost.
- GGRecon, review of *The Sunless City*, https://ggrecon.com/reviews/dark-souls-the-sunless-city-board-game-review: "Death sets you back to your bonfire, and while there you can improve your skills and level up" and "bonfires have a limit to how many visits you can make to them"; boss fights "felt tense". No comment on grind or on the cost of shopping.
- The Sixth Axis, *The Sunless City* review, https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2024/07/26/dark-souls-the-board-game-the-sunless-city-review/: calls the game "very punishing"; says nothing on sparks or shopping.
- BoardGameGeek threads (seen only as search-engine snippets, pages returned 403): "A newcomer's first impression of the game using the 1.0 and 2.0 rules" (https://boardgamegeek.com/thread/2967762/) and "Where to start with V1 Core Set?" (https://boardgamegeek.com/thread/3466763/). The snippets say: one poster disliked V2's "spending a spark to return to the bonfire and reset the market" and felt it "works more like a Homeward Bone from the video game than a bonfire"; another thought V2 has "very slightly better rules and a better campaign". I could not read the full threads, so treat these as unverified paraphrase of a search summary.
- 2017 baseline (V1), Tabletop Gaming review, https://tabletopgaming.co.uk/reviews/dark-souls-the-board-game-review: souls "serve as both currency for new equipment and the experience required to level up", progress comes from "grinding through battle after battle to earn souls", and the reviewer wanted "the idea of repetition leading to player improvement" but found it missing. This criticises the V1 loop that the V2 spark-per-visit rule was meant to damp.
- Tampere University PlayLab, "Dark Souls: The Board Game, Balancing Challenge" (https://www.tuni.fi/playlab/dark-souls-the-board-game-balancing-challenge/): could not be fetched directly; a search snippet quoted a commenter suggesting "exchanging a spark (bonfire reset) for double souls and thus eliminating the need to repeat the first round". Unverified.

Answer to "tension or grind?": from what I could read, reviewers who liked V2 praise the spark as a lives counter and as the cost of replaying; those who disliked it (community guide, BGG snippet) say the reset-and-replay loop is still grind, and one dislikes the spark being spent just to shop. Nobody I could read said that making levelling cost a spark restored the video game's push-your-luck feel specifically.

## 3. Community house rules

Sources: GameRant, "Dark Souls board game house rules", https://gamerant.com/dark-souls-board-game-house-rules/; community guide (section "V1 Common Complaints and House Rule Fixes"); Google Doc "1 Spark Variant for Shorter Play Times" by "Greema" (https://docs.google.com/document/d/11EuLdAClCHn84xpkhqWK5mbmKb_mrmHTqixGX2ADbAI/); r/DarkSoulsTheBoardGame "House Rules Megathread" pages (only search snippets; Reddit not fetchable).

Rules that gate or tax visits (closer to 2022):
- Community guide fix for unreliable drops: "Reveal 7 cards from the equipment deck to create a shop. Each item costs 1 soul. The shop is refreshed each time when sitting at the bonfire and using a spark." Reasoning given: curated items rather than blind draws. This ties the shop to a spark.
- Greema's 1 Spark Variant: start with one spark regardless of player count, "Spark is consumed upon death or resting"; treasure "cannot be purchased", it is earned by clearing rooms; Luck can be restored between encounters for 1 soul without a rest. Stated aim: "1 spark provides chance for a re-do or to gain a few more stat upgrades to be ready for that boss". Shortens play. This removes the shop and keeps levelling at a rest.

Rules that make free or reduce visits:
- "Double Souls / Half Sparks" (GameRant and community guide): double souls from encounters, half the sparks. Guide: "This effectively cuts the number of encounters played in half while keeping the amount of souls you get the same." GameRant's listed downsides: each spark is easier to use, and a four-player party gets one spark, "too harsh against tough bosses" for some. The aim was to remove grind.
- "Mastered encounters" (community guide): an encounter cleared without Heroic Action, Luck or Estus is not replayed; the party takes the souls and moves on. GameRant ("Farming Rooms") notes in campaign mode sparks can be bought at the price of one room, enabling auto-farming.
- "16 starting souls for everyone" (GameRant): so the party can level and shop before the first fight; reason given is that early encounters are slow when the party is "really underequipped".
- "Tiny Shop" (community guide): look at the top 2 cards when buying and keep one, to cut random draws.
- Subreddit snippet (unverified paraphrase): one commenter said that if you remove farming you must change soul rewards per player to balance it.

None of the house rules found separates levelling from shopping as the design question asks; none says "free shop, gated levelling". No house-rule author I could read argued for gating in terms of risk to carried souls.

## 4. Design theory on push-your-luck and banking

Sources here are mostly practitioner blogs; I did not reach Board Game Design Lab or the text of Engelstein's *Building Blocks of Tabletop Game Design*, so nothing below is quoted from either.

- Tabletop Gaming, "What are Push-Your-Luck Games?", https://tabletopgaming.co.uk/features/what-are-push-your-luck-games: "You have the option to stop, to chicken out and call it a day when the odds get too tough"; "you will never know if you stopped at exactly the right time" keeps players engaged; in Quacks of Quedlinburg the risk shifts as tokens come out, so players recalculate each draw; safe spots in 13 Beavers let players wait while others take risks.
- Don't Eat the Meeples, "Ten great push your luck games", https://www.donteatthemeeples.com/p/ten-great-push-your-luck-games: the essential feature is a meaningful stop-or-continue choice; in Quacks, "you're risking moving one step closer to exploding... You'll feel it"; Deep Sea Adventure balances treasure against air ("striking that balance is tough"); in Can't Stop a costly over-roll that loses progress is balanced by the occasional careless roll that leaves you in "a very strong position".
- Don't Eat the Meeples, "Risk as an essential feature in games", https://www.donteatthemeeples.com/p/risk-as-an-essential-feature-in-games: risk can fail to create tension when agency is absent ("Risk is a non-factor in games like this because player agency itself is a non-factor", on Snakes and Ladders); stakes can be made clear by what an opponent might do. Does not discuss banking directly.
- Skeleton Code Machine, "Giant Enemy Crab", https://www.skeletoncodemachine.com/p/giant-enemy-crab: "I certainly wouldn't just slap push your luck mechanisms everywhere, as they run the risk of adding too much randomness"; notes busting early makes the downside feel worse.
- Search summaries (unverified, from retailer/glossary pages): Incan Gold's single decision is to continue or leave, made simultaneously; Can't Stop punishes pressing on by losing the turn's progress; Deep Sea Adventure uses a shared air supply and carried treasure that slows you. Clank!: reviewers say the most valuable treasures sit deepest and risk escalates as the dragon's anger rises (Casual Game Revolution and others, via search summary).
- Jamie Madigan, Psychology of Games, "Level drain and loss aversion in D&D", https://www.psychologyofgames.com/2022/06/level-drain-and-loss-aversion-in-dd/: citing Geoffrey Engelstein, "losing something is more painful than gaining something of equivalent value is pleasurable"; D&D removed level drain over editions. Engelstein's *Achievement Relocked* (MIT Press, 2020) is the reference text for loss aversion in design (catalogue entries only; not read).
- Darkest Dungeon (video game, not the board game), via search-result review snippets (PC Gamer, Kotaku and others): deciding whether to continue or "return to town with my earnings was always challenging, and often led to disaster in 'just one more room'"; retreat means "you don't make much money" and characters return with stress. The same loot-versus-return structure; no board game source found.
- Bloodborne board game, Kingdom Death, Slay the Spire: not found with relevant commentary in the searches I ran (searches returned only the Darkest Dungeon video game). Not covered.

Failure conditions: the sources say push-your-luck fails when the decision has no real stop option, when odds are opaque or exact enough to solve (an unsourced blog point surfaced by search, flagged as unverified), when losses are felt as arbitrary, and when too much randomness swamps agency. The specific case of free banking is not discussed in any source I could read; the inference that it removes the tension is mine (see section 6).

## 5. Commentary on the video games' bonfire loop

- Wikipedia, "Bonfire (Dark Souls)", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonfire_(Dark_Souls): bonfires are "a place of rest and form of in-game checkpoint"; resting "will respawn most enemies" (bosses and mini-bosses stay dead); bonfires are "a utility area for leveling up"; on death you respawn at the last bonfire without your souls and lose them permanently if you die again before recovering them.
- Game Developer, "Cutting through the fog of Dark Souls, part one", https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/cutting-through-the-fog-of-dark-souls-part-one-: souls level you up and buy items; the author argues the risk/reward balance is off ("far easier to lose" than to gain); resting "will revive all dead enemies"; because bonfires are far apart you often fight back through enemies. The article does not say levelling is limited to bonfires.
- Game Developer, "Dark Souls game design analysis: why do we come back to die", https://gamedeveloper.com/design/dark-souls-game-design-analysis-why-do-we-come-back-to-die-: the Fire Keeper is "the only person that levels you up!" (as quoted); central principle "the higher the difficulty, the better the reward". Does not discuss soul loss.
- Meta Weta blog, "How Dark Souls turned bad ideas into genius", https://themetaweta.blogspot.com/2020/02/how-dark-souls-turned-bad-game-design.html: souls are "used both for leveling up your character and purchasing equipment"; on death you lose carried souls; "keeps players always wanting more souls"; the walk back is "a very slow, grindy, repetitive experience".
- matchstickeyes, "Difficulty in Demon's Souls: what we can learn from behavioural finance", https://www.matchstickeyes.com/2011/11/20/difficulty-in-demons-souls-what-we-can-learn-from-behavioural-finance/: loss aversion makes the penalty feel harsher than it is; the author is "always careful to return to safety and spend my souls" once they have enough, which keeps the amount at risk small. This is a direct account of the bank-or-press-on decision.
- Search-result summaries (unverified): finding a new bonfire sharply lowers the sense of risk in the surrounding area (one Game Developer-hosted piece); Dark Souls II's removal of levelling at bonfires frustrated some players (Steam Community thread; not read).

## 6. What this implies for the design question

This section uses only the evidence above. Where I infer, I say so.

### Option A: everything free between encounters (the 2017 rule)

For:
- It is the official 2017 text, so the existing materials assume it.
- Only a rest costs a spark, so sparks are not spent on shopping or levelling alone; the spark dial stays a measure of resets and defeats.
- Matchstickeyes' account of the video game (spend souls as soon as you have enough, so little is at risk) shows that banking early is a legitimate and common play pattern, not a bug.
- Several house rules (16 starting souls, tiny shop, double souls) exist because early play was underpowered and slow; free access helps there.

Against:
- Souls are only at risk inside an encounter, so carrying them is nearly free. By inference from the push-your-luck sources (a stop option must carry a cost to be a real decision), the bank-or-press choice largely disappears. No source tested this directly.
- Publisher stated the optimal V1 play was farming with every spark before the boss.

### Option B: free shop, levelling only at a rest

For:
- No source argues for this split; it is untested in the evidence. The only arguments are inferences: levelling is the larger sink (2, 4, 8 souls per step) so gating it concentrates the banking decision on the biggest spend, while a free shop (1 soul per item) keeps gear changes cheap.
- Greema's variant and the "Spark: Luck for 1 soul without resting" detail show that small restores outside a rest already exist in the game.

Against:
- No evidence found that either designers or players have tried it, so its effect on carried-soul risk is unknown. Equipment is often the main spend by count, so a free shop would still leave souls un-banked if the party spends them on items.

### Option C: both only at a rest (the 2022 rule)

For:
- It is the official revised rule, and the publisher chose it after observing farming. Each visit costs a spark, so banking is a priced decision. That is the structure the push-your-luck sources describe (a real stop option with a cost).
- Resetting encounters on each return makes a visit expensive in time as well, which reviewers read as "the core Dark Souls feeling" (BoardGameQuest) and a lives counter (GGRecon).
- The video game works the same way: levelling at the Fire Keeper or bonfire, enemies respawning on rest, souls lost on death (Wikipedia; Game Developer).

Against:
- Community guide: grind "is still very much present"; difficulty dropped partly through exploitable rules without shortening play; a BGG poster disliked paying a spark to refresh the market.
- Double Souls / Half Sparks, Mastered encounters and Farming Rooms are all fixes aimed at this exact grind, and the four-player half-spark variant is called too harsh by some.
- Loss aversion sources warn that loss is felt more than gain, so a harsh price may feel punitive (inference).
- Soul loss is lighter in this ruleset than in the video game's "lost on second death" if souls simply sit on a failed encounter card; the tension depends on that rule more than on the shop rule (inference).
